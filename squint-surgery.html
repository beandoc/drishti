Squint Surgery

Advanced surgical correction to align eye muscles and restore binocular vision.

When to Operate?

The timing for squint surgery is decided based on the patient's age and type of deviation:

  • Restore 3-D Vision: The primary advantage of operating early is the restoration of good binocularity (3-D vision).
  • Consistent Measurements: In some cases, surgeons prefer to wait until a child is slightly older to obtain consistent and highly accurate muscle measurements.
  • Adult Intervention: Adults can undergo surgery at any age. Those with intermittent squints have a high chance of regaining full 3-D vision if treated early.
  • Expert Decision: The precise timing is best decided by your surgeon based on squint type, age, and presence of a lazy eye.

Important Considerations

  • Dynamic Process: Overacting muscles are weakened and weak muscles are strengthened without compromising blood supply.
  • Post-Op Redness: Redness of the eye is normal and may persist for about a month.
  • Eye Pressure: Pressure inside the eye may temporary increase, requiring medication and regular follow-up.

Facts About Squint Surgery

Alignment vs Vision

Squint surgery corrects the **alignment** of the eyes. It does not improve visual acuity (clarity) itself.

Recurrence Risks

In eyes with very low vision, there is a possibility of the squint recurring. The timing of recurrence is difficult to predict.

Post-Op Therapy

Regular follow-ups and exercises are essential. Refractive correction (glasses) should be continued to maintain good vision and binocularity.

What Happens During the Procedure

1

Anesthesia

At Nav-Drishti, surgery is done on an outpatient basis. Local anesthesia is used for adults, while general anesthesia is used when operating on children.

2

Muscle Adjustment

An instrument (lid speculum) holds the eye open. The surgeon detaches part of the muscle and moves it to a new position to align the eyes correctly.

3

Dissolvable Stitches

Muscles are fixed with stitches hidden behind the eye. These dissolve on their own over a few weeks.

After Surgery Care

The eye may be sore for a few days, often feeling like "grit" or sand in the eye. This is normal and can be managed with simple painkillers.

Immediate Effects

Watery eyes or small amounts of blood in tears (lasts a few weeks).

Recovery Side Effects

Itchy eyes from stitches or temporary double vision as the brain adjusts.

Returning to Activities

  • Daily Tasks: Watch TV or read as soon as you feel able.
  • Work/School: Usually safe to return after about 1-2 weeks.
  • Direct Contact: Do not wear eye makeup, drive, or swim for at least 2-4 weeks.
  • Hygiene: Avoid any activity that puts too much pressure on the eye or involves direct contact.

Understanding the Risks

While complications are rare, it is important to be aware of the following risks before proceeding:

Under/Over Correction

Further surgery might be needed to fully align the eyes, especially in severe cases.

Permanent Double Vision

Very rare, but may require special prism glasses to correct.

Infection or Cysts

Build-up around the eye that might require antibiotic treatment.

Slipped Muscle

Muscles slipping out of position requires immediate surgical correction.
